From Warfare to Welfare: Leaders, Eras, and the Evolution of Policy Attention

Oscar Calvo-González, Axel Eizmendi and Germán Reyes

We study policy attention, that is, the priorities that presidents emphasize, using 1,149 annual speeches to congress from ten Latin American countries between 1819 and 2021. Policy attention moved from War and Peace and Public Administration in the nineteenth century to Infrastructure in the mid-twentieth century and to Social Welfare in recent decades. We also find that policy attention shifts sharply when a new president takes office, especially after coups and after changes in ideology.

How did policy attention change over two centuries?

In the nineteenth century, War and Peace and Public Administration took most of each speech, while the new republics fought wars and built their administrations. Infrastructure grew from the 1900s and stayed above 30 percent of the average speech from the 1940s to the 1960s. Economic Development peaked in the 1980s, and Social Welfare has been the largest topic since the 2010s.

Each of these shifts took several decades and many presidents. This raises the question of whether presidents shape these priorities or follow them, which we turn to below.

What happens when a new president takes office?

Policy attention shifts sharply. In the paper, the shift is larger after coups than after elections, and larger when the new president's ideology differs from the predecessor's.

What these results cannot show

Speeches record what presidents chose to tell congress. In the paper, topic shares move with government spending and economic growth, but these correlations do not show that a speech caused a policy.

The collection is uneven. Some countries and years have no speech, and some countries start late, such as Paraguay in 1881 and the Dominican Republic in 1967.

The topic model counts words. It cannot tell whether a president praised or criticized what the words describe.

Brazil's speeches are in Portuguese, and the topic model uses the Spanish texts, so the charts leave Brazil out.

Notes for researchers

How the topics were estimated

We estimate a topic model (latent Dirichlet allocation) on the Spanish texts. Thirty-three topics minimize perplexity on held-out speeches. Six topics reach a decade-average share above 20 percent at some point, and the other 27 never pass 10 percent in any decade; the charts group those 27 as Other topics. A language model labeled each topic from its most likely words. In the paper, the topic shares agree with an independent classification by a language model, with correlations from 0.26 to 0.63.

Sources and verification

The speeches come from national congresses and their libraries, online repositories, news articles and printed volumes, some held at the U.S. Library of Congress. We converted scanned speeches to text with optical character recognition, and a member of the team checked every converted text by hand. The catalogue records the source and the verification notes of each speech.
